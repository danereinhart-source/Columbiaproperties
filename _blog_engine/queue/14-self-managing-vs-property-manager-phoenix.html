Plenty of rental owners in Phoenix manage their own properties, and some do it very well. Others start out self-managing, get worn down by late-night calls and tenant problems, and eventually hand it off. Since we are a property management company, you might expect us to say everyone should hire a manager. That is not true. The right choice depends on your time, your temperament, how close you live to the property, and how many units you own.

Here is an honest look at the tradeoffs.

Time: More Than Just Collecting Rent

When a tenant is in place and everything is working, a rental can feel almost passive. The time demands show up in bursts: during vacancy and leasing, when something breaks, at lease renewal, and at move-out. In a single turnover, you might be scheduling cleaners and repairs, taking listing photos, answering inquiries, running showings, processing applications, writing the lease, and doing a move-in inspection. That can easily eat several evenings and weekends.

Ask yourself whether you have that time available, and whether you would rather spend it on your job, your family, or other investments.

After-Hours Calls

Air conditioners do not fail on a convenient schedule. In Phoenix, an AC outage in July is an urgent issue for the tenant's health and safety, and it often happens in the evening when the system has been running hard all day. Burst supply lines, roof leaks during monsoon storms, and lockouts tend to land at inconvenient hours too.

If you self-manage, you are the emergency contact. That means having reliable vendors who will actually answer the phone after hours, and being willing to pick up yourself.

Vacancy and Pricing

Every month a property sits empty is a month of lost rent. Vacancy is often the biggest hidden cost of self-management, especially if the rent is set too high, the listing is weak, or showings are hard to schedule around a full-time job. On the other side, pricing too low leaves money on the table every month for the length of the lease.

A professional manager prices off current local comparables and has systems for marketing and showings. That does not guarantee a faster lease, but it removes many of the common reasons rentals sit. Our post on the cost of tenant turnover in Phoenix breaks down how vacancy adds up.

Screening Risk

Tenant screening is where self-managing owners can take on the most risk without realizing it. A single bad placement can lead to months of unpaid rent, property damage, and a formal eviction. At the same time, screening has to be done consistently and in line with fair housing laws. Applying different standards to different applicants, or asking the wrong questions, can create legal exposure even when the intent is good.

Good screening means written criteria, applied the same way to every applicant: verifying income, checking rental history with previous landlords, running credit and background checks, and documenting your decisions.

Legal Compliance

Arizona has specific landlord-tenant rules, and some of them trip up owners who only deal with them every few years. A few examples you can count on: Arizona security deposits are capped at one and a half months' rent unless the tenant voluntarily prepays rent, and state law prohibits cities from enacting rent control. There are also specific notice requirements and timelines for entry, repairs, deposit returns, and lease violations, and the eviction process has its own procedures. If you are not confident you know the current rules, talk to an Arizona landlord-tenant attorney before you need one.

Taxes have changed too. Since January 1, 2025, Arizona cities can no longer charge transaction privilege tax on residential rentals of 30 or more consecutive days. That is one less filing for many long-term landlords, but it does not apply to short-term rentals, so check with your CPA if your situation is mixed.

Vendor Pricing and Quality

Individual owners often pay retail rates for repairs and may not know which vendors are reliable. Property managers typically work with a set of vetted vendors and have a sense of what a fair price looks like for common jobs in the area. That can help with both cost and quality, though you should always ask a manager how they select vendors and whether they add any markup.

Distance

If you live across town, self-managing is workable. If you live in Flagstaff, Tucson, or another state, it gets harder fast. Someone has to let in the plumber, check on the property after a storm, and do inspections in person. Long-distance owners often end up paying for a lot of one-off help anyway, which narrows the cost gap with full management.

An Illustrative Cost Example

Here is a simple example, not a prediction for your property. Say your Phoenix rental leases for $2,000 per month and your manager charges an 8% monthly management fee. That works out to $160 per month, or $1,920 over a year of collected rent. Placement and renewal fees would be additional, depending on the manager.

Whether that is worth it comes down to what you get back. Weigh the fee against things like:

  • The value of your own time, including evenings and weekends during turnovers
  • The cost of an extra week or month of vacancy if a self-managed listing sits longer
  • The potential cost of a bad tenant placement
  • Repair costs and vendor pricing
  • Peace of mind, especially if you live far away

For some owners, the math clearly favors self-managing. For others, avoiding even one bad placement or a long vacancy covers the fee many times over. Only you can make that call, and our pricing page lays out exactly what we charge so you can run your own numbers.

Who Should Self-Manage

Self-managing can be a good fit if you:

  • Live close to the property and have flexible time
  • Enjoy the hands-on work and are comfortable with conflict
  • Are handy or already have reliable, responsive vendors
  • Are willing to learn and keep up with Arizona landlord-tenant rules
  • Have enough cash reserves to handle surprises without stress

A property manager is usually the better fit if you live out of the area, have a demanding job, own several properties, or simply do not want to be the person tenants call at night.

How to Evaluate a Property Manager

If you decide to hire help, do not choose on fee alone. Ask how they screen tenants, how they price and market vacancies, how they handle maintenance approvals and after-hours emergencies, what the owner statements look like, how and when you get paid, and how easy it is to cancel. Ask for the company's Arizona Department of Real Estate license number and look it up yourself. Our guide on how to choose a property manager in Phoenix has a full list of questions.
