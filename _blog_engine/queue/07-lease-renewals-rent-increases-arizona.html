Ask experienced landlords what drives long-term returns and many will give the same answer: keeping good tenants. A renewal means no vacant weeks, no make-ready, no marketing, and no gamble on someone new. Yet renewals are often handled at the last minute, with a rent number pulled from thin air and a quick email a few weeks before the lease ends.

Here is a better approach for Arizona rental owners, from timing the conversation to pricing the renewal to putting it on paper.

Start the Conversation Early

As a practical habit, we suggest reaching out to tenants about renewal 60 to 90 days before the lease ends. That is not a legal requirement. It is simply a window that works. It gives the tenant time to decide without feeling rushed, and it gives you time to market the home if they plan to leave.

Starting early has other benefits:

  • You learn their plans sooner. If the tenant is buying a house or relocating, you can line up showings before the home goes vacant.
  • You avoid a summer scramble. In the Phoenix area, leases that end in July and August can be harder to fill. Knowing early lets you plan, or offer a lease term that shifts future expirations into a busier season.
  • You can address issues. A renewal conversation is a good time to ask if anything in the home needs attention. Fixing a small item can make the difference between a renewal and a move-out.

Before you reach out, read your current lease. Many leases include specific language about renewal, notice to vacate, and what happens if neither side acts before the end date. Your lease terms control a lot of this, so know what yours says.

Arizona Has No Rent Control

Arizona state law prohibits cities from enacting rent control. That means no city in Arizona, whether Phoenix, Tempe, Tucson, or Flagstaff, can cap how much you raise the rent at renewal. You set the renewal rent.

That freedom comes with a responsibility to use it well. Just because you can raise rent by any amount does not mean you should. The goal is the number that produces the best result for the property over time, and that usually involves weighing the increase against the cost of losing the tenant.

Pricing a Renewal: Increase vs. Turnover

When a tenant leaves, the costs add up quickly. There is the vacancy itself, often a few weeks or more. There is cleaning, paint touch-ups, carpet cleaning or replacement, minor repairs, and in the Phoenix heat, often some landscaping work. There are leasing and marketing costs. And there is the risk that the next tenant is not as reliable as the one you had. We break this down in our post on the cost of tenant turnover in Phoenix.

Here is an illustrative example. Say your home rents for $2,000 a month and a comparable home nearby is listed at $2,150. Raising the rent by $150 brings in an extra $1,800 over a twelve-month lease. If that increase pushes the tenant to move, and the home then sits empty for one month, you have lost $2,000 in rent before counting any turnover costs. In that scenario, a smaller increase that keeps the tenant may come out ahead.

That does not mean you should never raise the rent. It means you should know the market and make a deliberate choice. A few guidelines:

  • Check current comparables. Look at similar homes in the same area that have actually leased recently, not just asking prices. Our guide on how to price your Phoenix rental explains how.
  • Factor in the tenant's track record. A tenant who pays on time, takes care of the home, and rarely calls with problems is worth keeping. Many owners price renewals for strong tenants a bit below full market.
  • Consider the condition of the home. If the home is due for updates, it may not command top-of-market rent anyway.
  • Avoid surprise jumps. If rent has been below market for years, a series of moderate increases is often easier for a good tenant to absorb than one large one.

Offering Options

Some owners give tenants a choice of renewal terms. For example, a 12-month renewal at one rate and a shorter term at a slightly higher rate. This can help you line up lease expirations with stronger leasing months, and it gives the tenant a sense of control. Just make sure each option is clearly spelled out in writing.

Put Every Change in Writing

Once you and the tenant agree, document it. A renewal should be a signed written agreement, whether that is a new lease or a written lease extension or addendum. It should state:

  • The new lease start and end dates
  • The new monthly rent and the date it takes effect
  • Any changes to other terms, such as pet rent, utilities, late fees, or who handles landscaping or pool service
  • That all other terms of the original lease remain in effect, if that is the case

Verbal agreements create disputes. A tenant who remembers a different rent number or thinks a term changed when it did not is a problem you can avoid with a signed document. Keep a copy in your records and give one to the tenant.

If your lease has a security deposit, remember Arizona caps deposits at 1.5 months' rent, unless the tenant voluntarily prepays rent. If you raise the rent and want to increase the deposit, keep that cap in mind and document any change. Our post on security deposits in Arizona has more.

Month-to-Month Considerations

Sometimes a lease ends and the tenant stays on a month-to-month basis, either because the lease converts automatically or because no one signed a renewal. Month-to-month gives both sides flexibility, but it also means less certainty. The tenant can leave on fairly short notice, and you have a harder time planning.

If you are raising rent or ending a month-to-month tenancy, the amount of notice you must give is set by your lease and Arizona law. Check both before you send anything, and if you are unsure, ask an attorney. Getting the notice period wrong can delay a rent increase or complicate an exit.

Many owners find it simpler to offer a fresh fixed-term lease rather than letting tenancies drift into month-to-month. If you do allow month-to-month, some owners charge a modest premium for the flexibility, which should be set out in writing.

When a Tenant Says No

If the tenant decides not to renew, get their notice in writing and confirm the move-out date. Then shift into turnover mode: schedule a pre-move-out walkthrough, plan the make-ready, and start marketing. A careful move-out inspection protects you when it is time to account for the deposit.
