Sooner or later, almost every landlord has a month where the rent does not show up on time. Sometimes it is a bank delay or a tenant who forgot. Sometimes it is the first sign of a bigger problem. How you respond in those first few days matters. A clear, consistent process protects your income, keeps you on the right side of Arizona law, and gives you options if things do not improve.

This guide walks through grace periods, late fees, the 5-day notice, documentation, and payment plans. It is general information, not legal advice. Before filing any eviction, talk to an Arizona landlord-tenant attorney.

Grace Periods: What Your Lease Says Is What Counts

Many owners assume Arizona law gives tenants a few extra days to pay. It does not. State law creates no grace period for rent. If your lease says rent is due on the 1st, it is due on the 1st.

Any grace period comes from the lease itself. Plenty of landlords include one, often a few days, because it reduces friction over small delays like a holiday weekend or a slow bank transfer. If your lease includes a grace period, honor it exactly as written. If it does not, rent is late the day after it is due.

Whichever approach you choose, be consistent. Treating one tenant differently from another, or waiving the rules some months but not others, invites disputes.

Late Fees: In Writing and Reasonable

In Arizona, a late fee must be set out in the written lease, and it should be reasonable. If the lease does not mention a late fee, you generally cannot add one after the fact. Your lease should state:

  • When the fee kicks in (the day after rent is due, or after any grace period)
  • How the fee is calculated, whether a flat amount, a daily amount, or both
  • Whether there is any cap on total late fees

What counts as reasonable is not a fixed number in the law, and a fee that looks punitive can be challenged. If you are writing or updating a lease, have an attorney or experienced property manager review the late fee language. Then apply it the same way every month.

Step One: Reach Out Quickly

When rent is late, a short, polite message on the first late day often solves the problem. Many late payments are simple mistakes. A text or email that says the payment has not been received, asks when to expect it, and notes the late fee under the lease is usually enough.

Keep the tone professional. You are not accusing anyone. You are confirming facts and reminding the tenant of the terms they agreed to. Keep a copy of every message.

Step Two: The 5-Day Notice

If rent is still unpaid, the next step in Arizona is a written notice. For nonpayment, a landlord must give the tenant a written 5-day notice before filing an eviction. The notice tells the tenant how much is owed and that the lease may be terminated if it is not paid. You cannot file an eviction until the notice period has ended.

How you deliver the notice affects when the clock starts:

  • Hand delivery: the 5 days are calendar days counted from when the tenant receives the notice.
  • Certified mail: the period runs from the date the tenant signs the green card, or 10 days after the postmark, whichever comes first.

Mistakes in the notice, such as a wrong amount, missing information, or improper delivery, can get an eviction case dismissed and send you back to the start. This is one place where it is worth having an attorney review your forms and process. Our Arizona eviction process guide covers what happens after the notice period in more detail.

Document Everything

Good records are your best friend if a late payment turns into a dispute or an eviction. Keep:

  • A rent ledger showing each charge, payment, and late fee, with dates
  • Copies of every text, email, and letter to and from the tenant
  • A copy of the 5-day notice, with proof of how and when it was delivered (a signed delivery record, a certified mail receipt, or the green card)
  • Notes from phone calls, with the date and what was discussed
  • Any written payment agreement

If you end up in court, a judge will want to see a clear timeline. Owners who keep organized records from day one are in a much stronger position than those trying to reconstruct events from memory.

Payment Plans: Pros and Cons

Sometimes a reliable tenant hits a rough patch. A job loss, a medical bill, or a car repair can leave someone short for a month. A payment plan can be a reasonable response, but go in with your eyes open.

Potential upsides:

  • You may recover the full amount without the cost and time of an eviction.
  • You keep a tenant who has otherwise been good, and avoid vacancy and turnover costs.
  • It builds goodwill with a tenant who is trying to make things right.

Potential downsides:

  • If the tenant cannot catch up, you may simply be delaying the inevitable while the balance grows.
  • Accepting partial payments can affect your ability to move forward with an eviction based on an earlier notice. Talk to an attorney about how partial payments interact with your notice before you accept them.
  • It can set an expectation that late rent will always be negotiable.

If you agree to a plan, put it in writing. List the amounts, the due dates, what happens if a payment is missed, and confirm that current rent must also be paid on time. Both parties should sign it.

When to Call an Attorney

If the 5-day notice period passes and the tenant has not paid or reached an agreement with you, consult an Arizona landlord-tenant attorney before filing. Eviction procedures are technical, the rules change from time to time, and court filing requirements need to be followed precisely. An attorney can confirm your notice was proper and handle the filing correctly.

Prevention: The Best Fix for Late Rent

The easiest late payment to deal with is the one that never happens. Two habits help the most.

Thorough Screening

A tenant's rental history, income, and track record with past landlords are the best predictors of how they will pay you. Verify income, call prior landlords, and look for patterns rather than single blips. Our post on tenant screening best practices explains what to check. Rushing to fill a vacancy with a weak applicant often costs more than a few extra weeks of vacancy.

Autopay and Easy Payment Options

Make it easy to pay on time. Online payment with automatic recurring payments removes the most common excuse, forgetting. Send a reminder a few days before the due date. Make sure the lease spells out accepted payment methods and the due date clearly.

Clear Expectations at Move-In

At lease signing, walk the tenant through the due date, the grace period (if any), the late fee, and how to pay. Tenants who understand the rules from the start are more likely to follow them.
