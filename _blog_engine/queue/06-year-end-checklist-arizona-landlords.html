Most rental owners think about taxes in March or April, when the CPA starts asking for documents. The owners who have the easiest tax season are the ones who did a little work in November and December. Year-end is the natural moment to close out the books, catch anything you missed, and set your property up for a better year ahead.

This checklist is not tax advice. Think of it as a list of things to gather and questions to bring to your CPA.

1. Pull Together a Full Year of Income Records

Start with what came in. For each property, you want a clean record of every rent payment, late fee, pet rent, and any other charge the tenant paid during the calendar year. If you held a security deposit, note it separately. A deposit you are holding is generally treated differently from rent, and your CPA will want to know if any portion was applied to damages or unpaid rent during the year.

If a tenant moved out mid-year, make sure the move-out accounting and any final charges are documented.

2. Gather Every Expense Receipt

Next, the money that went out. Go through bank and credit card statements month by month and match each rental-related charge to a receipt or invoice. Common categories include:

  • Repairs and maintenance: plumbing calls, HVAC service, appliance repairs, pest control, landscaping.
  • Insurance: landlord policy premiums, and any umbrella coverage tied to the rental.
  • Property taxes: your county tax bills (Maricopa, Pima, Coconino, Yavapai, and so on).
  • HOA dues and fees: including any special assessments.
  • Utilities you paid: water, trash, or any service the lease leaves in your name.
  • Management and leasing fees: monthly management, placement, and renewal fees.
  • Mortgage interest: your lender's year-end statement usually arrives in January.
  • Professional fees: CPA, attorney, and similar costs related to the rental.

Separate true repairs from larger improvements, like a new roof, a replaced HVAC system, or a full kitchen remodel. Improvements are usually handled differently from repairs on a tax return, so flag them for your CPA rather than lumping everything together. Our post on Phoenix landlord tax deductions walks through the common categories in more detail.

3. Log Your Mileage

If you drove to the property to meet a contractor, show the home, or pick up supplies, those trips may matter at tax time, but only if you have a record. If you did not keep a log, reconstruct one now from your calendar, texts, and receipts: date, destination, purpose, and miles. Owners who drive up to a cabin rental in Flagstaff or Prescott a few times a year can rack up more miles than they expect.

4. Sort Out Vendor 1099s

If you paid contractors, handymen, landscapers, or other service providers for work on your rental, you may be required to issue them a 1099 form after year-end. The reporting thresholds, the specific form, and the filing deadlines have changed in recent years, and the rules depend on how the vendor is structured and how you paid them. We are not going to quote numbers here. Ask your CPA which vendors need a form and when it is due.

What you can do now is make the job easy:

  • Make a list of every vendor you paid during the year and the total paid to each.
  • Note whether each vendor is an individual, an LLC, or a corporation, and how you paid them (check, bank transfer, card, payment app).
  • Request a W-9 from any vendor you do not already have one for. It is much easier to get in December than in late January.

If a property manager paid vendors on your behalf, ask how they handle vendor reporting so nothing falls through the cracks.

5. Prepare for Schedule E

Most individual owners report rental income and expenses on Schedule E of their federal return. Organizing your records in the same general categories makes your CPA's job faster. Aim for one summary per property, with income at the top and expenses grouped by category. If you own more than one rental, keep them separate.

6. Have the Depreciation Conversation

Depreciation is one of the more valuable parts of owning rental property, and also one of the most commonly misunderstood. The building (not the land) and certain improvements are generally depreciated over time, which can offset rental income on paper. If you bought a property this year, placed a new one in service, or made major improvements, bring the closing statement and invoices to your CPA and ask how they should be treated.

This is also a good time to ask your CPA whether there are any planning steps that make sense before December 31, rather than after.

7. Understand the TPT Change

For years, many Arizona cities charged a transaction privilege tax (TPT) on residential rental income, and landlords had to hold a license and file returns. That changed on January 1, 2025. Under SB 1131, signed in 2023, Arizona cities can no longer charge TPT on residential rentals of 30 or more consecutive days. The Arizona Department of Revenue automatically cancelled TPT licenses that only listed the residential rental code.

A few things to keep in mind:

  • Any TPT owed for periods before 2025 still applies. If you had an older balance or a late filing, it did not go away.
  • The exemption does not cover hotels or transient lodging. If you operate a short-term rental, you are in a different situation. See our post on short-term vs. long-term rentals and confirm current requirements with your CPA.
  • If you still hold a license for other business activity, check with your tax professional about what you need to file.

8. Review Your Insurance

Pull out your landlord policy and read the declarations page. Does the dwelling coverage still reflect what it would cost to rebuild today? Is loss of rent coverage included? Are your liability limits where you want them? Also confirm your lease still requires tenants to carry renters insurance, and that you have current proof of coverage on file.

9. Look at Rent Levels for the New Year

Year-end is a good time to check whether your rent is still in line with the market. Look at which leases expire in the first half of the new year and start thinking about renewal pricing early. Rent that is well below market leaves money on the table every month. Rent that is too high risks turnover and vacancy, which usually costs more than a modest increase brings in. Our guide on how to price your Phoenix rental covers the details.

10. Set (or Refill) Your Reserve

Every rental needs a cash cushion for the repair you did not plan on. In Arizona, that is often an AC compressor in July or a water heater that gives out without warning. Decide on a reserve amount per property and fund it. Some owners think in terms of a few months of rent; others set a fixed dollar figure based on the age of the roof, HVAC, and appliances. The right number depends on your property, but having a number at all puts you ahead of many owners.
