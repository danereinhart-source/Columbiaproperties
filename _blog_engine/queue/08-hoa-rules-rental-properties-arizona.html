Many homes in the Phoenix metro sit inside a homeowners association. Master-planned communities in Gilbert, Chandler, Peoria, Goodyear, and Scottsdale, along with condo complexes across the Valley, all come with a set of rules. If you rent out a home in one of these communities, you are dealing with a third party in the landlord-tenant relationship. The HOA does not care who signed the lease. If the rules are broken, the owner gets the letter.

Here is what Arizona rental owners should understand about HOA rules, what associations can and cannot require, and how to keep tenants and the association on the same page.

What Arizona HOAs Can Require From Landlords

Arizona law puts limits on what an HOA can demand when you rent out your home. An association may require limited information about your tenants, specifically:

  • The names and contact information of adult occupants
  • The lease start and end dates
  • A description of the tenants' vehicles and their license plate numbers

In age-restricted communities, the HOA may also require identification showing date of birth, so it can confirm the community's age requirements are met.

An HOA may also charge an administrative fee for a new tenancy, but that fee can be no more than $25. It applies to new tenancies, not lease renewals with the same tenant.

What HOAs Generally Cannot Do

Outside of that $25 administrative fee and recreational-facility fees, Arizona HOAs generally cannot charge rental units differently from owner-occupied units. In practice, that means an association should not be adding a separate rental surcharge to your dues just because you lease the home out.

The information an HOA can require is also limited to the list above. If your association is asking for copies of your full lease, tenant credit reports, or other information beyond what the law allows, it is worth asking which rule they are relying on. If the disagreement continues, talk to an attorney who handles HOA matters.

CC&Rs Can Still Restrict Leasing

Here is the part that catches owners off guard. Even with the state protections above, your community's CC&Rs (covenants, conditions, and restrictions) can still limit how you rent. Common restrictions include:

  • Minimum lease terms: some communities require leases of at least six months or a year.
  • Short-term rental bans: many associations prohibit nightly or weekly rentals entirely.
  • Registration requirements: you may need to submit the tenant information listed above within a set time after a lease starts.
  • Leasing the whole home only: some CC&Rs prohibit renting individual rooms.

Read your CC&Rs, bylaws, and rules before you list the home, and again if the association amends them. If you are thinking about a short-term rental strategy, the CC&Rs may decide the question for you, regardless of what city law allows. Our post on short-term vs. long-term rentals in Phoenix covers that decision in more depth.

Pass the HOA Rules to Your Tenant

Your tenant cannot follow rules they have never seen. The simplest fix is to attach the HOA rules and regulations to the lease as an addendum and have the tenant sign or initial it. The lease should also state clearly that the tenant must comply with the HOA rules and that the tenant is responsible for any fines caused by their violations.

Rules that commonly trip up tenants in Arizona communities include:

  • Trash and recycling bins: many HOAs require bins to be stored out of sight except on pickup day.
  • Parking: limits on street parking, overnight parking, RVs, boats, trailers, and commercial vehicles.
  • Landscaping: weeds in desert landscaping, dead plants, and overgrown trees are frequent violation triggers, especially after monsoon rains.
  • Exterior changes: holiday decorations left up too long, items stored on patios or balconies, or anything that changes the look of the home.
  • Pets and noise: leash rules, pet waste, and quiet hours.

When the HOA updates its rules, send the tenant a copy and keep a record that you did. If a dispute comes up later, being able to show the tenant received the rules matters.

Violation Letters and Fines: Who Pays?

The HOA will send violation notices and fines to the owner of record, which is you. The association generally has no direct relationship with your tenant, so you are responsible to the HOA for paying fines and correcting violations, even if your tenant caused them.

Whether you can pass the cost to the tenant depends on your lease. If the lease makes the tenant responsible for HOA fines caused by their conduct, you can bill the tenant. If it does not, you may end up absorbing the cost. That is one more reason to have clear lease language.

A practical approach when a violation letter arrives:

  • Read the notice carefully and note the deadline to correct the issue.
  • Contact the tenant right away with a copy of the notice and a clear request.
  • Follow up to confirm the issue is fixed, and document it with photos.
  • If the violation is the owner's responsibility (a roof issue, exterior paint, a dead tree in the front yard that the lease says you maintain), handle it promptly.
  • Respond to the HOA in writing if needed, and keep copies.

Unpaid fines and repeat violations can escalate, so do not let notices sit.

Gate Access, Amenities, and Move-In Logistics

HOA communities often come with gate codes, remotes, key fobs, pool keys, and amenity access cards. These can turn a simple move-in into a frustrating one if they are not handled in advance. Before a new tenant moves in:

  • Confirm how many gate remotes, pool keys, and mailbox keys you have, and list them in the move-in paperwork.
  • Find out whether the HOA needs tenant registration before it will issue new codes or activate access cards.
  • Check whether the community restricts move-in times or requires notice for moving trucks.
  • Record all keys and devices handed over, so you can account for them at move-out. Our move-in and move-out inspections guide covers this.

Also be aware that some HOAs suspend amenity access for an owner's account if dues are past due or violations are unresolved. That affects your tenant, so keep your account current.

Keep Your HOA Account in Good Standing

Your tenant pays you rent; you pay the HOA. Set up automatic payment for dues if your association allows it, keep a calendar of any special assessments, and watch your mail (or email) for HOA notices. If you live out of state, make sure the association has a current mailing address for you or your property manager.
