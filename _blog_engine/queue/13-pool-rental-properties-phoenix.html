In the Phoenix area, a backyard pool is a real selling point. When summer temperatures sit well above 100 degrees for weeks at a time, many renters actively look for a home with one. But a pool also adds maintenance, equipment, utility costs, and liability that a home without one simply does not have. If you own a rental with a pool, or are considering buying one, here is how to handle it so it stays an asset rather than a headache.

Decide Who Maintains the Pool

This is the most important decision, and it should be settled in writing before the tenant moves in. There are two basic options: the tenant takes care of the pool, or the owner provides a professional pool service.

In most cases, we recommend a professional pool service paid for by the owner and built into the rent. Here is why:

  • Consistency. A weekly service keeps chemistry balanced and the equipment checked, regardless of how busy or experienced the tenant is.
  • Protecting expensive equipment. Pumps, filters, heaters, and automation systems are costly to replace. Poor chemistry and neglect shorten their life.
  • Fewer disputes. If a tenant-managed pool turns green, you end up arguing over who caused it and who pays to fix it. With a professional service, there is a record of every visit.
  • Early warning. A pool tech who visits every week often spots leaks, failing equipment, or other problems before the tenant mentions them.

Whichever approach you choose, spell it out in the lease or a pool addendum: who handles weekly cleaning, who buys chemicals, who maintains water level, who pays for repairs, and what the tenant must report and how quickly.

Chemicals and Water Quality

Pool chemistry in Phoenix takes attention. Intense sun burns off chlorine quickly, high temperatures encourage algae, and our hard water tends to cause scale and calcium buildup on tile and equipment. Over time, dissolved solids build up as water evaporates and gets topped off, and the pool may eventually need to be partially or fully drained and refilled.

If tenants handle any part of the chemistry, give them clear written instructions and make sure chemicals are stored safely, away from children and out of direct sun. Many owners prefer to keep chemicals out of tenant hands entirely and let the pool service handle all of it.

Equipment

The pump, filter, and any heater, salt cell, or automation system are where the big repair bills live. A few practical steps:

  • Have the equipment inspected before a new tenant moves in and document its condition with photos.
  • Keep records of equipment ages, models, and past repairs so you can plan for replacements instead of being surprised.
  • Consider whether a variable-speed pump makes sense when the old one fails. Check with your utility about any current incentives.
  • Make sure the equipment area is easy to reach and that the timer is set to run the pump enough in summer.

Summer Evaporation and Water Level

Phoenix pools lose a lot of water to evaporation in the summer, especially during hot, dry, windy stretches. If the water drops below the skimmer opening, the pump can draw air and run dry, which can damage it. Someone needs to be checking the level and topping it off regularly.

An automatic fill valve can help, but it also hides leaks, since the pool quietly refills itself and the only sign may be a higher water bill. If the water bill jumps or the pool seems to be losing more than normal, have it checked for a leak. A simple bucket test can help tell evaporation from a leak, and a pool professional can confirm.

Monsoon Season and Debris

Monsoon storms bring wind, dust, leaves, and sometimes heavy rain that can overflow the pool or wash dirt and debris into it. After a storm, the pool may need extra cleaning, a filter cleaning, and a chemistry adjustment. Strong winds can also blow patio furniture or umbrellas into the water.

Before the season starts, trim trees and shrubs near the pool, make sure the deck drains are clear, and confirm the pool service knows to check in after major storms. Our monsoon season prep guide covers the rest of the property.

Liability and Insurance

A pool increases the liability exposure of a rental property. Drowning and injury risks are real, and they extend to tenants, their guests, and children in the neighborhood who might get into the yard.

Talk with your insurance agent about how your landlord policy treats the pool, whether your liability limits are high enough, and whether an umbrella policy makes sense. Some insurers have specific requirements or exclusions for pools, diving boards, and slides. It is also reasonable to require tenants to carry renters insurance with liability coverage. Make the requirement clear in the lease and collect proof of coverage before move-in.

Barriers, Fencing, and Safety Equipment

Pool barrier and fencing requirements in Arizona vary by city and county. Rules can cover fence height, gate latches, self-closing gates, door alarms, and whether the house wall can count as part of the barrier. Confirm the ordinance that applies to your specific address with your city or county before you lease the home, and check again if you make changes to the yard.

Beyond what is required, it is worth inspecting at every turnover that:

  • Gates self-close and self-latch properly
  • There are no gaps, damaged sections, or climbable objects next to the fence
  • Any door or window alarms that are installed actually work
  • Pool covers, if used, are in good condition

Use a Pool Rules Addendum

A pool addendum to the lease sets expectations and documents that the tenant received safety information. Common items include:

  • Who is responsible for which maintenance tasks
  • No glass containers in the pool area
  • Children must be supervised at all times
  • Gates must stay closed and latched
  • No diving if the pool is not designed for it
  • Report equipment problems, leaks, or water level issues promptly
  • Do not change equipment settings or add chemicals unless the lease says the tenant handles them

Have an attorney or experienced property manager review your addendum so it fits Arizona law and your situation.

Pricing the Pool into the Rent

A pool usually lets you ask more rent than a comparable home without one, and it can shorten vacancy during spring and summer leasing. But the premium needs to cover the real cost of ownership: weekly service, chemicals, extra water, higher electricity for the pump, repairs, and possibly higher insurance.

For example, if you plan to include a weekly pool service, add up that monthly cost along with the other pool expenses and compare it to the rent difference you see between homes with and without pools in your area. If the premium does not cover the cost, the pool is lowering your return, not raising it. Our guide on how to price your Phoenix rental walks through comparing similar properties.
